Comparing this plot with Figure 2.6, we observe that the computed step response closely matches the shape and asymptotic behavior shown in Figure 2.6, where the response initially rises and gradually approaches a steady-state value around 2 as t→10.

Impulse Response Comparison:

The second plot displays the impulse response h(t) obtained using MATLAB’s impulse function. It starts at 1 and decays exponentially towards zero, which aligns with the behavior expected from an impulse response.

The third plot, representing the exact impulse response (derived as the derivative of the step response s(t), also exhibits an exponential decay pattern, closely matching the computed impulse response in the second plot.

Summary and comments：

Through this MATLAB lab experiment, I gained a deeper understanding of several important properties of convolution, especially in the context of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems. First, I observed that for time-invariant systems, convolution accurately represents the system's response, producing consistent results whether calculated directly or through iterative methods. However, for time-varying systems, convolution may not capture all dynamic changes, as it inherently assumes time invariance. Additionally, I learned about the overlap-add method as an efficient way to compute the convolution of long sequences by breaking them into smaller segments. This method, by dividing the input signal into blocks, convolving each block separately, and then combining the results, maintains accuracy while reducing computational load. Overall, this experiment demonstrated how convolution techniques can be adapted based on system properties and computational efficiency requirements, enhancing my understanding of convolution’s application in practical signal processing.
