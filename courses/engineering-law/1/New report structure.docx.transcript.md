31.1 The University's degrees and other academic awards are given in recognition of a student's personal achievement. All work submitted by students for assessment is accepted on the understanding that it is the student's own effort.

31.2 Plagiarism is defined as the submission or presentation of work, in any form, which is not one's own, without acknowledgement of the sources. Plagiarism includes inappropriate collaboration with others. Special cases of plagiarism can arise from a student using his or her own previous work (termed auto-plagiarism or self-plagiarism). Auto-plagiarism includes using work that has already been submitted for assessment at this University or for any other academic award.

31.3 The incorporation of material without formal and proper acknowledgement (even with no deliberate intent to cheat) can constitute plagiarism.

Work may be considered to be plagiarised if it consists of:

a direct quotation;

a close paraphrase;

an unacknowledged summary of a source;

direct copying or transcription.

With regard to essays, reports and dissertations, the rule is: if information or ideas are obtained from any source, that source must be acknowledged according to the appropriate convention in that discipline; and any direct quotation must be placed in quotation marks and the source cited immediately. Any failure to acknowledge adequately or to cite properly other sources in submitted work is plagiarism. Under examination conditions, material learnt by rote or close paraphrase will be expected to follow the usual rules of reference citation otherwise it will be considered as plagiarism. Departments should provide guidance on other appropriate use of references in examination conditions.

31.4 Plagiarism is considered to be an act of fraudulence and an offence against University discipline. Alleged plagiarism, at whatever stage of a student's studies, whether before or after graduation, will be investigated and dealt with appropriately by the University.

31.5 The University reserves the right to use plagiarism detection systems, which may be externally based, in the interests of improving academic standards when assessing student work.

Identifying group membership and differentiation factors (up to 1 page)

PetCare1.0 vs Dextronix:

Points of Parity: Both products are designed to manage basic cardiac pacing for pets, ensuring heart rhythm stability.

Points of Difference: PetCare1.0 stands out with its battery-free and wireless design, which eliminates the need for battery replacements, while Dextronix pacemakers require periodic replacements. Additionally, PetCare1.0 includes optogenetic control, providing advanced control of the heart rhythm, whereas Dextronix focuses on simplicity and affordability without MRI compatibility or advanced monitoring.

Dextronix pacemakers are best suited for customers looking for economical, basic cardiac solutions, whereas PetCare1.0 is ideal for owners of small pets who need high-tech, long-lasting solutions. Moreover, Dextronix lacks advanced features like remote monitoring and automatic heart rate adjustments, making it less adaptive compared to PetCare1.0.

PetCare1.0 vs Medtronic:

Points of Parity: Both PetCare1.0 and Medtronic's pacemakers, such as Azure™ MRI SureScan™, offer MRI compatibility and Bluetooth-based remote monitoring for continuous health monitoring.

Points of Difference: PetCare1.0 excels with its battery-free design, eliminating the need for surgical battery replacements every few years, whereas Medtronic's devices have a long, but limited, battery life (typically up to 10 years). PetCare1.0 also features optogenetic control, allowing for more nuanced heart rhythm management, which is not available in Medtronic pacemakers.

Medtronic’s product, however, is designed for wider usage across different sizes of pets, making it more versatile in application, but PetCare1.0 specifically targets smaller pets, such as birds and puppies, with a lighter weight and smaller size (110 mg). Medtronic's pricing, due to its advanced technology and global brand recognition, can be higher, making PetCare1.0 a more specialized choice for small pet owners looking for a non-invasive, long-term solution.

PetCare1.0 vs MicroPort Alizea:

Points of Parity: Both products integrate MRI compatibility, Bluetooth-enabled remote monitoring, and long-lasting functionality (13 years for MicroPort, potentially 20 years for PetCare1.0 due to its battery-free design).

Points of Difference: PetCare1.0 differentiates itself with its battery-free technology, reducing the need for replacement surgeries, whereas MicroPort relies on a traditional battery-powered system. PetCare1.0 is also tailored to smaller animals, providing a lighter, smaller device suited for rodents, birds, and small puppies. Moreover, PetCare1.0’s use of optogenetic control adds an innovative dimension to heart regulation, enhancing its performance for delicate heart conditions.

On the other hand, MicroPort’s Alizea has a more established presence in human and veterinary cardiology markets, and its focus on sleep apnea monitoring and advanced data tracking offers unique additional health insights. While MicroPort aims for high-tech, comprehensive patient care, PetCare1.0 excels in offering a specialized, compact, and non-invasive solution specifically for smaller pets.

MoSCoW Analysis (Up to a page)

The MoSCoW analysis is a way of prioritizing your user requirements to focus your product's design process. “Must have” requirements are those most critical to the design and will be the focus of your first prototype. These include regulatory requirements as well as your product's main intended use. “Should have” features are important, but not necessary to solve the problem and can be addressed in secondary prototypes. “Could have” requirements are addressed in final prototypes and include desirable features that can improve customer satisfaction and user experience. “Would like” are all the features that would make your product exceptional but may not be practical at this time.
