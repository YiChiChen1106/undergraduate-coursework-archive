Ⅰ. Overall System Design Approach

Our team designed and implemented a modular navigation system to enable autonomous UAV operation in GPS-denied and cluttered indoor environments. The navigation system architecture was composed of several tightly integrated subsystems: environmental perception, state estimation, local path planning, and flight control.

For environmental perception, we adopted the Intel RealSense D430 stereo depth camera, which provided real-time 3D information of the UAV’s surroundings [1]. This allowed our system to continuously map obstacles and generate high-fidelity representations of the environment, which were essential for safe navigation.

State estimation was achieved through the VINS-Fusion framework, which combined stereo visual input and IMU data to deliver accurate six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) pose estimation [2]. This ensured reliable UAV localization and trajectory tracking, even in challenging or feature-sparse conditions.

Local path planning was handled by the EGO-Planner module, which computed smooth, collision-free trajectories by leveraging local grid maps built from sensor data[3]. EGO-Planner enabled the UAV to avoid obstacles dynamically and optimize its flight path in real time.

The flight control subsystem was based on the PX4 autopilot and MAVROS interface, enabling tight feedback between planned trajectories and low-level actuation. This setup guaranteed responsive, stable, and precise maneuvering in response to high-level navigation commands.

To streamline system deployment and operation, our team developed a unified startup script that sequentially launched all necessary nodes and synchronized data flow between modules.

By coordinating these subsystems, our navigation system allowed the UAV to autonomously perceive, plan, and execute complex flight missions—including waypoint navigation, obstacle avoidance, and safe landings—within unknown indoor environments.

Ⅱ.Experimental Design – Subsystem Design and Solution(s)

Establishing Data Pathways and Vision Nodes Between RealSense and UAVs

The team began by preparing the software environment on the onboard Orange Pi computer running ROS. We installed the librealsense2 SDK, VINS-Fusion, and the realsense2_camera ROS package. The Intel RealSense D430 stereo camera was connected to the system to serve as the main sensor for visual perception and depth data acquisition.

One-Click Startup Script

To streamline system initialization and improve repeatability, we developed a unified one-click startup script. This script automatically launched all required software nodes in the correct order: the RealSense driver, MAVROS interface, PX4 autopilot, and VINS-Fusion state estimator. Built-in delays between steps ensured that each module had enough time to initialize before the next one started. This automation enabled the entire vision-to-state estimation pipeline to be reliably launched with a single command, significantly increasing experimental efficiency and system reliability.

Checking for VINS-Fusion Odometry Drift

After the vision nodes were launched, we routinely monitored the IMU pre-integration and odometry output from VINS-Fusion in real time. The team would gently move the UAV and then return it to its original position, checking whether the reported position returned to its initial value. If cumulative drift exceeded acceptable limits, we recalibrated sensors and restarted the initialization process to ensure state estimation accuracy.

Launching the Controller Node

Once visual localization and state estimation were established, the team proceeded to launch the autonomous flight controller node. We integrated open-source PX4ctrl code, adjusted necessary parameters such as PID gains, speed limits, and topic remappings, and ensured the controller could receive odometry data from VINS-Fusion. Successful launch and communication with the flight controller were confirmed by real-time system feedback, enabling robust autonomous flight in our experiments.

Launching the Ego-Planner Local Path Planner

With the flight controller operational, we launched the Ego-Planner module for local trajectory optimization and dynamic obstacle avoidance. Using a dedicated launch file, we brought up all required nodes and set parameters such as map size, maximum velocity, and acceleration to match the test environment. This enabled real-time, collision-free path planning tailored to our specific testing conditions.

Launching RViz Visualization to Monitor Flight and Grid Map

For real-time monitoring, we used RViz to visualize the grid map, live UAV trajectory, obstacle points, and current UAV pose. This allowed the team to intuitively observe system behavior, obstacle avoidance, and the progress of each flight mission during testing.

Developing and Running the Autonomous Waypoint Program

Based on measured site dimensions, we designed a sequence of 3D waypoints and saved them in a configuration file for repeatable experiments. The autonomous waypoint navigation program enabled the UAV to take off, visit each waypoint in sequence, and land automatically. The program workflow included: issuing automated takeoff and landing commands, loading all waypoints from the configuration file, sequentially sending each target to the flight controller and pausing as required, and ensuring a fully autonomous mission workflow without manual intervention once started.

This integrated and automated pipeline significantly improved the reliability, efficiency, and safety of our UAV experimental missions.

Ⅲ. Results

A. USB Connection Stability and Camera InitializationDuring early system deployment, our team encountered frequent "control transfer returned error" messages when connecting the RealSense D430 camera to a USB 3.0 port. Initially suspecting loose cable connections, we reinforced both cable ends with glue but failed to resolve the issue. After several unsuccessful attempts, one of our team members switched to a USB 2.0 port, which completely eliminated the errors. We later concluded that USB 3.0's stricter requirements on cable quality and signal integrity likely caused these issues.

B. State Estimation Accuracy: Visual Feature Dependence and Altitude Measurement 

During testing, the team observed that state estimation accuracy was strongly influenced by the visual richness of the environment. In particular, when the UAV operated in visually sparse settings—such as empty classrooms—significant localization drift would occur. Yichi Chen analyzed that in these scenarios, VINS-Fusion was forced to rely more on IMU pre-integration, which resulted in the accumulation of integration errors and reduced overall accuracy.

The team also encountered persistent altitude estimation issues. Initially, the UAV's z-axis readings remained inconsistent, puzzling the group for some time. After further investigation, Yichi Chen and Hancheng Zhou together identified the root cause: the PX4 flight controller's EKF2_HGT_MODE parameter was set to barometer by default, causing altitude measurements to depend on barometric data rather than visual input. By collaboratively changing the EKF2_HGT_MODE parameter to vision mode, they completely resolved the issue. After this adjustment, the UAV's altitude estimates became consistent with its actual flight altitude, greatly improving vertical positioning performance and the overall reliability of autonomous navigation.

C. Launching px4ctrl, Ego-Planner, and RViz We successfully launched the px4ctrl node, Ego-Planner, and RViz visualization without technical issues. Each component initialized correctly and provided the expected outputs, verifying that the perception, planning, and control subsystems operated as designed.

D. Running VortexEdge for Hovering and Point-to-Point Flight

During practical flight tests using the VortexEdge.py program, the team encountered and overcame several challenges. Initially, the UAV sometimes failed to take off or hovered unstably due to compatibility issues with certain PX4 firmware versions. This problem was resolved by reverting from version 1.14 to 1.13. Once autonomous flights began, the experiment was conducted with close teamwork and risk management.

Throughout each flight trial, one of the members was responsible for program execution and system monitoring. Another member handled the remote controller, remaining alert to intervene and trigger emergency landings whenever the UAV faced unexpected behavior or potentially dangerous situations, ensuring the safety of both the aircraft and the environment.

Additionally, two other members provided on-site support by standing ready with foam pads during each flight test. In the event of a forced or emergency descent, they were positioned to quickly move in and catch the UAV with the foam, minimizing the risk of physical damage to the drone upon landing. This collaborative approach allowed the team to conduct repeated autonomous flight missions safely and efficiently, achieving stable waypoint navigation and accurate landings while protecting the hardware throughout the testing process.
